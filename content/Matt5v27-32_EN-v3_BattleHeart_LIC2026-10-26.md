## 1. Sin begins in the heart, it' not merely in the act 罪始于内心，而不仅仅在于行为本身（5:27-28)

![[Point 1 - Sin Begins in the Heart.png]]
### a. Adultery is not merely an outward act.. (v27)

>27 “You have heard that it was said, ‘Do not commit adultery.’ 

We find the law against adultery in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:14 and Deut 5:18), where we read  “you shall not commit adultery”.

The Hebrew term for ‘adultery, נָאַף‎ (nā·ʾǎp̄) means to having “sexual intercourse with someone other than a spouse” (Swanson 1997, §5537). 

Adultery therefore “refers to a consensual sexual relationship between a married person and someone other than his or her spouse”[^3](Barry et al. (2016, § Matt 5:27) 

The Mosaic Law commanded that if a man or a women was found to commit adultery with his neighbor’s wife, ...he must be put to death (Lev 20:10). 

>“In several countries today, adultery remains a capital offense under laws influenced by conservative interpretations of Islamic Sharia. 
>
>Nations such as Iran, Saudi Arabia, Somalia, and parts of Nigeria and Pakistan retain legal frameworks where adultery—especially by married individuals—can be punished by death, often by stoning.[^4] 

In Jesus' day,  under Roman law,  people were not longer put to death for adultery.  

Yet this Mosaic stipulations communicated the seriousness of  adultery in God's eyes. 

What  however was the problem with the Jewish leaders' interpretation and teaching the law against adultery? 

Just as with the command against murder, the Pharisees and the teachers of the law conveniently narrowed the interpretation of the law to a mere physical act, overlooking the issue of lust in the heart.

John Stott (1985, 87 § Matt 5:27) explains: 

>“Once again the rabbis were attempting to limit the scope of the commandment you shall not commit adultery. 
>
>Although the sin of desiring another man’s wife is included in the tenth commandment against covetousness, they evidently found it more comfortable to ignore this. 
>
>In their view, they ...kept the seventh commandment, provided that they avoided the act of adultery itself. 
>
>They thus gave a conveniently narrow definition of sexual sin and a conveniently broad definition of sexual purity” (Stott 1985, 87 § Matt 5:27).


### b. Adultery takes place in the heart (v28)

Jesus therefore, carefully and systematically exposes the sin in the human heart. 

For just as there is **anger** beneath the sin of murder (v21-26), so we will see there is **lust** beneath the sin of adultery (vs 27-30), and “a **hard heart**” beneath divorce (verses 31-32). 

>28 But I tell you that anyone who **looks** at a woman lustfully has already committed adultery with her in his heart. 27「你们听见有话说：『不可奸淫。』 28只是我告诉你们，凡看见妇女就动淫念的，这人心里已经与她犯奸淫了。

Notice how Jesus shifts the focus from the act of committing, to the process of looking, lusting and commiting adultery in the heart.  


The GK  βλέπωa simply means ‘to see, to notice, to glance at" someone.  (LN 1996, 277 §24.7)

However the phrase "everyone who is looking" (πᾶς ὁ βλέπων), suggests a deliberate, sustained, or cultivating gaze rather than a merely incidental glance (Hagner 1993, 135; Davies and Allison 1988, 527).

Such purposeful look with the purpose of “of lusting" (πρὸς τὸ ἐπιθυμῆσαι αὐτὴν) turns into adultery in the heart [^5]

We live in a world where it’s challenging to go through even a single day without being bombarded with images or media that are intentionally crafted to to stimulate us sexually, or create sexual longing or desire.

Yet Jesus, is not here addressing such accidental attraction, but rather when an incidental look, turns into a purposeful and settled gaze - longing holds potential to become adultery in the heart. 

Jesus therefore exposes the covetous desires that lies beneath adultery, and demonstrates that sin begins in the heart before it manifests itself in outward action (Davies and Allison 1988, 526-28; Carson 2010, 146-47).

Being a disciple of Christ, and seeking God's kingdom and his righteousness, therefore is not merely about  “behavioral management”, or external obedience - instead it is about being transformed from the inside out - from within the heart.  

### c. The Example of David and Bathsheba (2 Sam 11:2-4)
Bible commentator RT France (2007, 204 § Matt 5:27-30) notes that the GK OT (LXX) use the same word ἐπιθυμέω (epithymeō lust) in 2 Sam 11:2-4 when David looked with lust at Bathseba, when he saw her half naked while bathing on her roof.  

To bath on the roof of a house in the coolness of the evening was a common practice at that time, and culturally David ought to have given her her privacy, especially in view that Bathsheba took a ritual bath of cleansing after her monthly period  (2 Sam 11:2-5).

It's very sobering to see how David, even as a man of God, transfixed by what he saw, casts off all restrain, and ignore the warning of his servant that Bathsheba was the wife of Uriah.  

Yet with the hook of lust firmly swallowed, David ordered to get her, so that he could get what he wanted. 

Any sympathy we might have for David for what initially seemed like an accidental glance quickly dissipates as we witness how he exploits his position of power, influence, and wealth to satisfy his desires and then conceal them.  

![[The Danger of Objectificationv1.png]]

From a women's perspective you have to see the horror, injustice of turning someone created in the image of God as merely an object for satisfaction.  

At the same time you wonder whether Bathseba could have been more discreet in protecting her privacy and not creating a stumbling block for those who might happen to gaze upon her.  

Scripture gives us no indication that Bathseba resisted or protested the Kings advances, which leaves us to wonder if she had been a willing participant in the adultery? 

We could further speculate that her husband’s prolonged absence may have made her both emotionally and physically vulnerable to the attention and advances of a man who made her feel special, beautiful, or desirable. 

The fact that he was a man of means and possibilities may have caused her to cast off her restraints as well. 

The point I am making is that whether due to lust that enters through the eyes of man, or whether by caused by loneliness, emotional neglect, or romantic longing - both men and women are vulnerable to to this sin of adultery that might strike at your heart unexpectedly. 

Jesus were frustrated with the Pharisees hypocritical handling of the adultery law, because the OT provided ample teaching against such lustful longing.  

To begin with the Tenth Commandment very clearly deals with this issue of covetousness when it says "“You shall not covet your neighbor’s house. You shall not covet your neighbor’s wife (10th)" (Exod 20:13-20).

An Israelite women certainly would have understood the law as "you should not covet your neighbors husband".  

The path to covetousness for a women might look different than for a man - perhaps longing for connection, intimacy, being listened, prospect of a good income and home etc...Yet it still enters the heart through what you see or hear, and is still covetous lust.  

The OT prophet Job is a good example for us as men. He remained vigilant by saying "I made a covenant with my eyes not to look lustfully at a girl (Job 31:1 NIV)."

Solomon, perhaps with his father David's sin in mind, urged his son not to lust in his heart after the beauty of the immoral women, nor to be captivated by her eyes, or by her smooth tongue (Prov 6:24-25)

![[Point 2 - Sin Demands Decisive Repentance (v29-30).png]]

It goes without saying that Jesus doesn’t actually want us to physically gouge out our eye for lust or cut off our hand for touching what’s forbidden.[^6]. 

His point is that our covetous and lustful desires are so serious that it's worth the considerable cost of stopping it both at the source and it's outflow into action [^7].  
## a. The self-reinforcing cycle of sin - explained (v29-30)

What is particularly striking is that Jesus does not simply command us to stop lusting in our heart.  

Instead, He specifically calls us to deal decisively with both the eye and the hand. 

> ..If your right eye causes you to stumble, gouge it out and throw it away..(v29)

>..And if your right hand causes you to stumble, cut it off and throw it away...(v30)

He addresses both the inflow into the heart and the outflow from the heart.

Why? A self-reinforcing cycle of sin can develop and continue for months or even years unless it is decisively broken. Notice the pattern in the diagram.


 The eye feeds the heart, and the heart directs the eye toward what it desires. 
 
 Those desires then move the hand to act, bringing a moment of satisfaction. 
 
 Yet that satisfaction does not extinguish the desire; it strengthens it. 
 
 The stronger desire seeks further stimulation through the eye, and the cycle begins again with increasing intensity.

This helps explain why Jesus speaks so forcefully about the eye and the hand. 

He is teaching His us as his disciples to take radical action against anything that either fuels sinful desires at their source or enables it's expression expression in practice.

## b.  The self-reinforcing cycle of sin - illustrated

To illustrate, imagine a man  entering midlife who has long dreamed of owning a powerful and expensive Harley Davidson motorcycle.

At first, the desire is relatively small. He begins watching videos, reading reviews, and browsing photos online. The more attention he gives it, the more the desire grows.

Before long, he visits a dealership, sits on the motorcycle, and perhaps takes it for a test ride. 

These experiences bring a measure of satisfaction, but they also deepen his longing. 

Gradually, his thoughts, conversations, time, and money begin to revolve around acquiring what his heart desires.

If he wants to break the hold that this desire has on his heart and mind, he must cut it off both the sources that feed it and the experiences that continually reinforce it.

## c.  The self-reinforcing cycle of sin - applied

Imagine you reconnect with an old friend or former boyfriend/girlfriend on social media.

At first, it seems completely innocent. You look at a few photos, read some updates, and wonder how life has turned out for them. But the more attention you give them, the more often they occupy your thoughts.

Before long, you find yourself checking their profile regularly, looking forward to new posts or messages, and imagining what life might have been like if things had turned out differently. 

These interactions bring a measure of satisfaction, but they also deepen the emotional attachment.

Gradually, your thoughts, emotions, and attention become increasingly focused on that person. 

What began as curiosity has grown into something far more powerful longing. 

This is why Jesus calls for radical action. Sin must be confronted both at its source and at its point of expression. 

**Whatever feeds sinful desire and whatever gives it opportunity to grow must be dealt with decisively[^8].**

_"The threat of lust is so strong and its dangers so great that a price comparable to the removal of eye or hand is not too great to pay as one seeks freedom from it... (Thomas 1998, §4624; Hagner 1993, 120; Stagg 1969, 109)

This might mean that your are reminded this morning that there might a person that you are giving too much thought, or allows secret longing.

## Summary and Application

Friends, firstly we have been reminded that 
### Sin begins in the heart, not merely in the act (Matt 5:27–28)

The religious leaders conveniently restricted the Seventh Commandment (_"Do not commit adultery"_) to physical acts of illicit intercourse. 

Jesus rejects this narrow definition, establishing that kingdom righteousness requires transformed motives and desires rather than superficial behavior management.

Jesus does not condemn us for the accidental glance or natural visual attraction, but warns that deliberate, sustained gaze cultivated for the explicit purpose of self-gratifying lust is already adultery. 

Such adultery of the heart is rooted in coveting what belongs to someone else. 

Lust objectifies an image-bearer created to be loved and honored, reducing them to an object to be consumed.

Secondly we have seen that 
### Sin in the heart demands decisive repentance (Matt 5:29–30)

Jesus uses the shocking hyperbole—gouging out an eye or cutting off a hand—not to command physical self-mutilation, but to demand radical action against whatever traps or ensnares us (_skandalizō_).

By addressing both the eye (what enters the heart) and the hand (what proceeds from the heart), 

Jesus calls us as His disciples to eliminate the environments, habits, media, and relationships that feed wrongful desires before they cause spiritual destruction.

Thirdly Jesus's teaching here prompts us to ask..

###  What then is the path of hope and restoration in the Sermon on the Mount?  

![[Application - the Path of hope.png]]

Whether our struggle is lust, covetousness, or some other misplaced desire, none of us can stand before these words and claim innocence.

Jesus exposes the hidden motives of our hearts not to leave us crushed under moralistic condemnation or hopeless self-effort. 

Instead there is much hope even within the Sermon on the Mount.  

Let's return to Jesus' opening words in the Beatitude: 

>Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. 
Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted... 
Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they will be filled"_ (Matt. 5:3-4, 6).

Jesus' teaching this morning has exposed the sin in every one of our hearts, yet he does not expose our sin in order to drive us away from Him. He exposes our sin in order to draw us to Him. 

From the very beginning of the Sermon on the Mount, He invites us to come as one - poor in spirit, mourning over our sin, and hungry and thirsty for His righteousness - the righteousness that we do not possess in ourselves.  

And to those of us who come to Him with repentant faith, Jesus makes wonderful promises - the kingdom will belong to us, we will be comforted,  and we indeed be filled or clothed with Christ's righteousness. 

Therefore, our hope is not found in our ability to conquer every sinful desire by our own strength. 

Our hope is found in Christ, who forgives sinners, who comforts the broken-hearted, and who give us HIs righteousness through faith in Him. 

So if Jesus' words have convicted you this morning, do not run from Him. Run to Him. 

Confess your sin, seek His mercy, and trust His grace. 

The One who exposes the sin of the heart is also the One who heals and transforms the heart.

And as we look to the closing words of the Sermon on the Mount, we hear Jesus say:

_"Everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man who built his house on the rock"_ (Matt. 7:24).

Instead of fixing our eyes on what we cannot have, Jesus calls us to fix our eyes on Him. I

nstead of allowing our hearts to be shaped by sinful desires, He calls us to build our lives upon His Word.

As we come to Christ in faith and humility,
listening to His voice, 
feeding on His truth, 
and putting His teaching into practice,
the Holy Spirit gradually transforms our hearts and minds. 

Old desires begin to lose their grip, and new desires begin to take their place. Our love for Christ grows stronger than our love for sin.

This is the hope of the Sermon on the Mount. 

Jesus does not merely expose the sin within us; He also provides the foundation upon which we can build a new life. 

The One who reveals the sickness of the heart is also the One who heals it. 

And as we build our lives upon Him, He graciously transforms us into the very people He calls us to be. Amen
