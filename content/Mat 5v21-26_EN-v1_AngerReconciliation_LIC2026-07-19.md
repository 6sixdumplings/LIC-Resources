# Matthew 5: 21-26 Anger and Reconciliation

# Introduction

A root of bitterness can very quickly grow when we allow hurts or wounds to fester in our hearts (Heb 12:15).  Bitterness easily spreads through our lives, poisoning our thoughts, emotions, words, and actions.  Our bitterness is shown through anger, resentment, and insults towards others. We should never allow such a root of bitterness to grow, nor to control our hearts or lives.  We now return to the Sermon on the Mount, where Jesus addresses anger (Matt 5:21-26).

# Background

In Matt 5:17-20, Jesus made it clear that he did not come to abolish the Law.  In fact, he came to give the Law its full and correct meaning, while also fulfilling all its requirements. Scripture presents Jesus as the New Moses, the true teacher and interpretor of God's holy law. 

In Matthew 5, we observe a repeating pattern: *“You have heard that it was said … but I tell you* …(Matthew 5: 5: 21, 27, 31, 33, 38, 43).

First, Jesus addresses two commands from the Ten Commandments—the prohibition against murder (5:21–26; cf. Exod 20:13) and against adultery (5:27–30; cf. Exod 20:14).

Next, Jesus turns to three societal issues rooted in the case laws of the Pentateuch, namely divorce (5:31–32; cf. Deut 24:1–4), oaths (5:33–37; cf. Lev 19:12; Num 30:2; Deut 23:21), and revenge/retaliation (5:38–42; cf. Exod 21:24; Lev 24:20; Deut 19:21).

Finally, Jesus culminates with the greatest command—love—expanding it beyond neighbour to include even enemies (5:43–48; cf. Lev 19:18).

The Jewish teachers of the law, ever since receiving the law on Sinai, often misinterpreted and misapplied God's law. They tended to interpret and apply God's law legalistically and externally, without seeing the spirit of the law or the law's heart intent.  Therefore, Jesus calls his disciples to a righteousness that exceeds the righteousness of the Pharisees. He calls us to a righteousness that extends beyond merely external obedience of the law. 

In the beatitudes (Matt 5:3-10), Jesus laid down the heart or inner attitudes such as meekness,  mercy, purity and peace upon which the law of God rests.  Matthew 5:21-48 is a profound portion of Scripture. Jesus corrects, reinterprets and intensifies the law in view of the inner attitudes of the Kingdom of God. 

Let's now look at Matthew 5:21-26, where Jesus reinterprets and intensifies the law against murder. 

**Let us read together Matthew  5:21-26**

>21 *“You have heard that it was said to the people long ago, ‘Do not murder, and anyone who murders will be subject to judgment.’ 
>
>22 But I tell you that anyone angry with his brother will be subject to judgment. Again, anyone who says to his brother, ‘Raca,’ is answerable to the Sanhedrin. But anyone who says, ‘You fool!’ will be in danger of the fire of hell*.

>23 *“Therefore, if you are offering your gift at the altar and there remember that your brother has something against you, 24 leave your gift there in front of the altar. First go and be reconciled to your brother; then come and offer your gift.*

>25 *“Settle matters quickly with your adversary who is taking you to court. Do it while you are still with him on the way, or he may hand you over to the judge, and the judge may hand you over to the officer, and you may be thrown into prison. 
>

# 1. Resentful anger is murder… (5:21-22)

Notice, firstly, how Jesus corrects the view of the teachers of the law who limited murder to simply the physical act of killing someone (v21).  

### 5:21a .. the people long ago..

**Read vs 21a**
>“**21a** You have heard that it was said to the people long ago, **21b** ‘You shall not murder, **21c** and anyone who murders will be subject to judgment.’

The phrase “to those of old” or the ancient ones (τοῖς ἀρχαίοις), refers to the Israelites who first received the Law at Sinai (Exod 20:13; Deut 5:17). These Israelites received God's law at Mount Sinai in the desert, and it was affirmed to them again before they entered the Promised Land (France (2007, § Matt 5:21, 200).

Upon receiving the Law at Sinai, the Levites were appointed to teach it to the people, a duty later entrusted to the Scribes (Deut 33:10; 31:9–13). Ezra the scribe, for example, devoted himself to the study, practice and teaching of God’s law. ([Ezra 7:10](https://www.bible.com/bible/111/EZR.7.10)) We read in Nehemiah 8:7-8 how he “gave the sense” of the Law so that the people may understand it ([Nehemiah 8:7-8](https://www.bible.com/bible/111/NEH.8.7-8))

Despite centuries of teaching and interpretation, the Pharisees and the teachers of the Law continued to apply a legalistic and external interpretation of the law.  Therefore, in this block of teaching (5:21-18), Jesus corrects how the OT laws were understood, applied, and taught. To Jesus' hearers, his teaching would have been shocking or even revolutionary.  

### 5:21b ..'You shall not murder'..

**Read vs 21b
>“**21a** You have heard that it was said to the people long ago, **21b ‘You shall not murder,** **21c** and anyone who murders will be subject to judgment.’

Beginning with the sixth command,  Jesus deals with the command against murder.  We observe immediately that the words *"you shall not murder"* are a direct quotation from [Exodus 20:13](https://www.bible.com/bible/111/EXO.20.13) and [Deuteronomy 5:17](https://www.bible.com/bible/111/DEU.5.17) in the LXX Septuagint (France 2007, § Matt 5:21, 200) . 

### 5:21b "..and anyone who murders will be subject to judgment.."

However, the second part of their teaching v21c is not a quote from OT scripture, but a summary of their application

**Read v21c**
>“**21a** You have heard that it was said to the people long ago, 21b ‘You shall not murder, **21c and anyone who murders will be subject to judgment.’**


To “*be subject to judgment* (Matt 5:21b) is a summary reference for the death penalty that was prescribed in the law ([Gen 9:6](https://ref.ly/Ge9.6); [Exod 21:12–14](https://ref.ly/Ex21.12-14); [Lev 24:17](https://ref.ly/Le24.17); [Num 35:30–31](https://ref.ly/Nu35.30-31) (France (2007, § Matt 5:21)

**Read  Genesis 9:6, Exodus 21:12 and Leviticus 24:17**

>**Genesis 9:6** Whoever sheds human blood, by humans shall their blood be shed; for in the image of God has God made mankind.

>**Exodus 21:12** Anyone who strikes a person with a fatal blow is to be put to death.

>**Leviticus 24:17** Anyone who takes the life of a human being is to be put to death.

Human life is valuable exactly because every person in the world was created in the image of God. Therefore, under the Mosaic Law the principle of ‘life for life’ demanded a death penalty for murder (Gen 9:6; Exod 21:23–25; Lev 24:17–20; Num 35:30–31; Deut 19:21). 

As a result, the teachers of the law summarised their theology as "anyone who commits a physical murder will be subject to capital punishment by a human court. [^3] While at first glance this might appear to be a correct interpretation of the Mosaic Law,  there were two problems with their interpretative framework: 

Firstly, the teachers of the law were faithful to the letter of the law, but they ignored its spirit.[^7]  They took the intended ultimate boundary of behaviour, namely murder and conveniently turned it into a very narrow definition of murder. They ignored the vast array of thoughts, emotions, words, and actions that eventually lead to murder. [^4][^5]

Secondly, they reduced the judgment of murder to a human court’s judgment, while ignoring the wrath and judgment of God upon the sin of murder.  Jesus, as the new Moses and true Levite, clearly opposes this legalistic and externalistic interpretation of the law. [^1] He expands the meaning of the law against murder to the inner recesses of the human heart.  Addressing the root issue of anger and resentment of the heart (Wiersbe 1996, 23)[^2][^6] [^8]. 

### Application

You might have expected that these early interpreters of God’s law, as its original recipients, would be expected to comprehend the law accurately.  Yet they failed to apply God’s law correctly, because they applied it legalistically, externally, while ignoring the thoughts and emotions of the heart. 

This invites us to approach Scripture, whether the OT or NT, with humility, because it's so easy to get it wrong. We must guard against mere external or legalistic obedience while failing to see the sin of our hearts. 

Also, as individual believers and collectively as the church of Christ, we must always return to Scripture. We must keep returning to the central matters of our faith. Making sure our interpretation is in line with the teaching of Christ, and our application in accord with sound doctrine (Titus 1:9; 2:1; 1 Tim 1:10; 6:3; 2 Tim 1:13; 4:3). 

# 2. Resentful anger will be judged (5:22).


### 5:22a ..**But I tell you...

**Read 5:22a** 
>**But I tell you** that anyone angry with his brother will be subject to judgment.

Notice the authority in Jesus' words, *but I tell you*! (5:22a). As the new Moses, Jesus is the true teacher, interpreter and fulfilment of God's law.   Green (2000, 94) notes that Jesus addresses the root of anger, not just the fruit of murder, going far deeper than the scribal tradition.

>“Jesus goes far deeper. He traces murder to its dark lair in human hearts: hatred. The God who sees in secret is affronted not only by the fruit, murder, but by the root, hatred" (Green 2000, 94).

It is not merely the act of killing a brother or sister that brings us under judgment, but even so small a matter as having anger and resentment against a brother or sister. Jesus means festering anger that flows from hurt and unforgiveness. Our hurt and anger escalate to resentment when we refuse to forgive. 

Our resentment turns negative thoughts into insulting words and public slander.  Our threatening words may well turn into physical action. 

Some translations of Matthew 5:22 include the phrase "*angry without a cause*". Stott (1985, 83 § Matt 5:22)[^9] points out that anger “without a cause” appears in many manuscripts but not in the earliest ones. He concludes that the phrase is a later addition, although he believes the scribes rightly understood Jesus’ intention.



### 5:22b ..anyone who says to his brother. 

**Read 5:22b**
>Again, anyone who says to **his brother,** ‘Raca,’ is answerable to the Sanhedrin.


We know that Jesus' audience included both his twelve disciples and the crowds following him.  So when Jesus refers to "*anyone who says to his brother*," he may primarily have in mind followers and disciples of Christ. Jesus is therefore setting the standard of Christian living for his disciples as citizens of the Kingdom of Heaven (France 2007, §Matt 5:22, 200)[^10]. 

We will see this theme of forgiveness and reconciliation re-emerge throughout Matthew's Gospel (France 2007, §Matt 5:22, 200)[^11]. 

### 5:22b ..Raca..

**Read 5:22b**
>Again, anyone who says to his brother, ‘**Raca**,’ is answerable to the Sanhedrin.

Raca means calling someone worthless, good-for-nothing or a fool (Fensham 1996, 998) [^12]. An angry, resentful heart often expresses anger in abusive words. Anger expressed in insult and slander is murder! [^13]

## 5:22b..is answerable to the Sanhedrin..

We must keep in mind that Jesus is speaking to his Jewish audience for whom the Sanhedrin was the highest legal court in Jerusalem  (Thompson 1996, 1060)[^14]. Jesus is making the point that angry insults ought to be treated with the same seriousness as physical murder in the supreme court 

**Read 5:22c**
>But anyone who says, ‘**You fool!’** will be in danger of the fire of hell


### 5: 22c ..‘You fool!..

Jesus emphasises that insulting a neighbour or fellow disciple is a serious matter.  When you demean someone in your heart, or express with your words that someone is an idiot, fool, moron or worthless -  it violates the law of God against murder. 

When you employ such angry words against your spouse or children,  your co-workers or supervisor, or someone who cut in before you while driving - you make yourself liable to be disciplined or judged by God.  

Always keep in mind that God created all people in his image. The Father loved and foreknew your fellow believers from before the creation of the world.  Christ redeemed them by his precious blood. The Spirit of God lives in them, and they are sealed for an eternal inheritance with you.  So who are you to judge, who are you to speak so abusively against your brother who will inherit eternity with you? 

Yes, he or she may have sinned and caused your hurt, but the chances are that you have done the same. We must avoid at all costs becoming hardened in our anger or resentment.  Repent of our angry heart and harmful words! Turn away from anger and insult, especially at home and among your fellow believers. 


It might be difficult for the modern reader to clearly distinguish the meaning of 'raca' and 'moron', but it clearly is a form of verbal attack that flows from an angry heart [^15].  It describes a downward spiral from anger towards assaulting someone’s intellect, to contempt for and verbal abuse of the entire person[^16][^17][^18]. 

Also notice the intensifying of liability from a local court to a supreme court, to judgment by God himself (v22c). 


**Read 5:22c**
>But anyone who says, ‘You fool!’ **will be in danger of the fire of hell**

## 5:22c ..in danger of the fire of hell..

Notice Jesus' stern warning that if we harden our hearts in anger and resentment, we find ourselves "*in danger of the fire of hell* (22c)". [^20] 

The fire of hell is a translation of the GK 'geënna',  that refers to the Valley of Hinnom.  The  Hinnom Valley was a burning rubbish dump outside and below Jerusalem.  Its constantly burning rubbish provided a vivid image of the eternal fire of hell prepared for the devil, his angels and unbelievers (Matt [25:41](https://ref.ly/Mt25.41)) (France 2007, 202 § Matt 5:22)[^19]

Jesus is not saying that once-off or occasional anger bring us into eternal judgement.  Instead he presents us with a very serious warning:  

Take care that anger, resentment and unforgiveness do not suffocate your faith.  Allow anger to grow, and it may consume your heart, mind, and emotions, suffocating your faith. You risk becoming like an unbeliever, and consequently, you risk the fire of hell. 

Our salvation depends upon our faith in Christ, and reconciliation with the Father.  Yet if your faith does not result in fruits of forgiveness and reconciliation, the genuineness of your faith is in question. Which means you are still at risk of the fire of hell (Green 2000, 94)[^23]. 

Stott (1985, 85 § Matt 5:22) pointedly draws out the connection between anger and murder.

>“Anger and insult are ugly symptoms of a desire to get rid of somebody who stands in our way. Our thoughts, looks and words all indicate that, as we sometimes dare to say, we ‘wish he were dead’. Such an evil wish is a breach of the sixth commandment” (Stott (1985, 85 § Matt 5:22)

Such hardened unforgiveness and anger demonstrate that you live in spiritual darkness, and are not in the light (1 John 3:15). 

Listen to the apostle John's warning: 

>"Anyone who hates his brother is a murderer, and you know that no murderer has eternal life in him" (1 John 3:15 NIV)

## What about righteous anger? 

We must also understand that not all anger is sinful, nor should we avoid conflict or problems.  God has given each of us the ability to experience hurt and anger, which are normal human emotions.  

Godly anger can help us discern when something is wrong or unjust.  We can use the energy of godly anger to resolve a problem, handle conflict and correct a wrong.  

However, righteous anger draws its energy from God's holiness, a pursuit of righteousness and justice, and is filled with God's love and mercy.  

Luther described righteous anger as an anger that is "friendly to the person but hostile to the sin" (Stott 1985, 84 § Matt 5:22)[^21]

Such godly anger looks very different to the resentful anger that Jesus deals with in vs 22. An ungodly settled anger is nursed inwardly, and feeds on malice, pride, vanity, hatred and revenge (Stott 1985, 84 § Matt 5:22)

Anger, resentment, and verbal abuse must be dealt with quickly and decisively, as we will see in vs 23-24 (Wiersbe 1996, 23)[^22]. 

## Review

Firstly, Jesus expands the command against murder to include the resentful anger and abusive slander

Secondly,  anger and insult are very serious in the eyes of God.  Our anger and harsh words to others make us liable before God and man to be judged or disciplined. [^34]

In vs 23-26, Jesus calls us to repent of our anger and slander. Rather than holding on to our anger, we should extend forgiveness and seek reconciliation.  

Jesus illustrates this message with two short examples or parables of reconciliation.  The first illustration involves reconciliation among brothers in a personal and religious context (vs 23-24).  The second deals with hostile relationships in a civil or legal situation (v25-26)  

Thirdly, we must...
# 3.  Reconcile with your brother (v23-24)


**Read 5:23-24**
>23a “Therefore, if you are offering your gift at the altar 23b and there remember that your brother has something against you, 24a leave your gift there in front of the altar. 24b First go and be reconciled to your brother; then come and offer your gift.


### 5:23a ..if you are offering your gift at the altar.. (v23-24)

Let’s consider Jesus’ audience before applying this section to ourselves. "Offering your gift at the altar (v23a)*" refers to any burnt offering,  sin offering or free-will offering (Lev 1-5) that the Jews would bring to the temple in Jerusalem to seek forgiveness of their sins or in thanks or worship of God[^24][^32]. 

Old Testament prophets such as Isaiah and Amos denounced the people's hypocrisy of meaningless sacrifices while living in broken relationships with one another (Isaiah 1:11-17, Amos 5:21-24).

For example, in Isaiah 1:13 we read: 
> "Stop bringing meaningless offerings! Your incense is detestable to me" (Isaiah 1:13 NIV)

God hated their sacrifices, sacred assemblies and feasts.  Although outwardly they were religious, inwardly their broken relationships showed that they were spiritually dead.  Broken relationships with others might be a sign of a broken relationship with God. 

**Read 5:23b**

>23a “Therefore, if you are offering your gift at the altar 23b **and there remember that your brother has something against you**,

### 5:23b ..and there remember that your brother has something against you..

In vs 21-22 Jesus dealt with the anger that we feel towards another.  Yet here in vs 23-24 anger flows in the opposite direction, the anger of a brother or a sister against us. 

The illustration paints a picture of the Israelite coming into the temple to make his offering on the altar before God. In that moment of quietness and reflection, right before the animal is sacrificed upon the altar,   he remembers that a fellow Israelite holds a grudge against him. Jesus doesn’t say if the worshipper is innocent or guilty.  Yet it's possible that there had already been a cycle of hurt, anger and slander between them, and the resentment is mutual. 

Well, should he continue to present his burnt offering before God?  Jesus says, no,  do not be a hypocrite like the Israelites of old! 



### 5:24a ..leave your gift there in front of the altar..

**Read 5:24a** 

>24a **leave your gift there in front of the altar.** 24b First go and be reconciled to your brother; then come and offer your gift.

Stop pretending to be reconciled with God when your life is full of broken relationships with others.  

The Galilean Jews who were listening to Jesus would have been intrigued by the implication of Jesus' teaching. Jesus delivered the Sermon on the Mount in Galilee.  For Galileans, going up to the temple to present their gift at the altar involved an 80-mile journey on foot to Jerusalem. Obeying Jesus' teaching would have meant a week's journey back to Galilee, the effort to reconcile, followed by yet another week's travel to finally offer your gift at the altar in good conscience (France 2007, 203 § Matt 5: 24).[^25]

Jesus likely used this illustration for dramatic effect, to stress the priority of reconciliation over mere religious observance.  Jesus urges decisive action to prioritise reconciliation over merely maintaining the religious status quo.  We cannot approach God in good conscience, expecting Him to forgive our sins,  while at the same time also harbouring unresolved anger and resentment. 

Jesus therefore calls us to decisive action, to be reconciled with our brothers and sisters [^26]. Jesus' teaching is also a vivid illustration of Paul's teaching in Eph 4:26 (France 2007, 203 § Matt 5: 24)[^27]

**Read Eph 4:26** 
>“In your anger do not sin”: Do not let the sun go down while you are still angry, and do not give the devil a foothold”


If Jesus were speaking into our context, I wonder what he may have said? He might point out that if you are about to worship at church, approach God in prayer, or holy communion,  and there remember that your brother or sister has something against you, be first reconciled. [^28][^33] Or if you are about to do your family devotions and there remember that you are angry with a family member, be first reconciled with your husband, wife or children.

Matthew Poole (1853, 23 § Matt 5:23-24) writes: 

>“leave your gift before the altar, and do what in you lies to be reconciled to your brother, to have a placable spirit to him, to purge your heart of wrath and malice, and any desire of revenge, and then come and offer your gift” (Poole 1853, 23 § Matt 5:23-24)

The apostle Paul likewise urges in 1 Tim 2:8 

>"I want men everywhere to lift up holy hands in prayer, without anger or disputing" (1 Tim 2:8 NIV)


Friends, the word of God invites us to quiet our hearts as we approach God.  Do you have unresolved anger or resentment towards anyone? Is there anyone who nurses a grievance against you?  How can you take the initiative to extend forgiveness and seek reconciliation?  

The Lord Jesus commands us this morning to take decisive action. What might it look like for you to be a blessed peacemaker (Matt 5:9) in this situation?  

 He warns us of the serious consequences of allowing anger and resentment to fester in our hearts.  

Whether you remember that someone holds something against you, or if you hold something against a brother or sister, you must take action to forgive and seek reconciliation (Mark 11:25) [^29]. 

>“And when you stand praying, if you hold anything against anyone, forgive him, so that your Father in heaven may forgive you your sins.” (Mark 11:25 NIV)

We must not allow our hurt and resentment to fester; we must deal with it honestly,  decisively and dail (Stott 1985, 85 § Matt 5:23; Wiersbe 1996, 23 § Matt 5:21-26) [^30][^31].  

> [!Prayer] Prayer
> Lord Jesus, have mercy on us. So often we cause hurt, but are not aware of the hurt we cause. Other times we are easy to take offense, quick to to demand justice or vengence, yet slow to ask for and give forgiveness. 


We have now covered that (1) resentful anger and slander is murder; (2) that anger and insults will be judged; (3) and that we must be reconciled with our fellow believers.  

Finally..

# 4  Settle matters with your adversary (v25)

In vs 25-26 we see the same principle reinforced, only in a different context.  The first example (vs 23-24) concerns a brother in the context of a place of worship, while the second example involves an adversary in the marketplace. Yet in both situations, someone holds something against you. 

The situation calls for immediate and decisive action to avoid more serious consequences (Stott (1985, 85 § Matt 5:23) [^35].

Read 5:25a
>25a “Settle matters quickly with your **adversary** who is taking you to court

In vs 25, the disciple of Christ faces a more serious and hostile conflict.   An accuser or adversary (ἀντίδικος antidikos)[^36]  emerges that desires to drag the disciple to court. This is therefore not a conflict that the disciple desires or to be dragged into; instead, it is forced upon him. 

We can easily think of examples of believers facing such conflicts that threaten legal action: A believer whose husband, after a season of conflict, hardens towards divorce. A Christian family faces financial misfortune and defaults on their home loan payment. The bank threatens to repossess their home. A business owner fails to deliver on an agreement or breaks a contract, resulting in a lawsuit from their client. 

**Read 5:25b** 
>25b “Do it while you are still together on the way, or your adversary may hand you over to the judge, and the judge may hand you over to the officer, and you may be thrown into prison.

Again, notice the legal terminology of being handed over (παραδίδωμι paradidomi) [^37]  and being judged (κριτής krites) in a court of law [^38].  It seems unlikely that Jesus’ purpose in this brief parable is to provide us with legal advice. Instead, he might simply be illustrating the importance of taking decisive action to reconcile with an accuser or adversary. Jesus calls us to take action to settle matters while there is still time,  before the consequences become more severe.  

France (2007, § Matt 5:25-27) emphasises the prudence of taking appropriate preventative action of settling outside of court. 

In conflict situations, there is usually a window of opportunity to resolve the conflict.  If you fail to make use of that window of opportunity to settle matters with your adversary, it will become more difficult or impossible to reconcile, and the consequences may become more serious. 

Even in Christian marriages, divorce can occur when anger is not dealt with.  Where a couple let anger, resentment and unforgiveness grow, the relationship becomes increasingly toxic until a point of no return is reached. 

**Read 5:26**
>26 I tell you the truth, you will not get out until you have paid the last penny.

Failure to take prudent action may lead to very significant negative outcomes. Having to pay the full debt (ἀποδίδωμι apodidomi)[^39], or bearing the full weight of the consequences.  

Jesus certainly does not desire for his disciples to be in and out of the court. As the salt of the earth and the light of the world (Matt 5:13–16; Mark 9:50; Luke 14:34–35; John 8:12), we want to avoid such serious conflict that will undermine our Christian witness. 

So Jesus urges us as his disciples to take extreme care to settle our conflict before it hardens and our adversary drags us off to court (Poole (1853, 24 § Matt 5:25-26).

Yet there may also be a deeper application here.  

**Read 5:26**
>26 I tell you the truth, you will not get out until you have paid the last penny.


In using the phrase "I tell you the truth" (v26), Jesus may be indicating that there might be a higher purpose of this warning. He may be reminding the crowds - those who have not yet become followers of Christ- that ultimately God is your adversary.  Your sin, anger, and broken relationships bring you under the judgement of God.  

It therefore is prudent,  and it calls you to decisive action to settle matters with God, and to be reconciled with him through faith in Christ - so that your sins may be forgiven.  If you ignore this window of opportunity to settle matters with God, to be reconciled with him through the Lord Jesus Christ. You will have to bear the full consequences of your sin, and pay the full price of your sin in eternal hell. 

 Jesus therefore urges us to make sure that we have settled matters with God, our adversary.  He urges us to be reconciled with Father by bringing our debt of sin to the Lord Jesus Christ in faith.[^40].
